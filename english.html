A descent in seven stops

Belowthe 24thFloor

Every morning, I take the lift down from the 24th floor of my Shenzhen apartment. Every few levels it brakes hard—the same shove as a hybrid ride-hailing car, complete with the driver's hair-oil smell. Both make me carsick. I have not had breakfast and already want to throw up.

— the body resigns before the mind does.
G / 08:17

Someone else's
better life.

On the office skybridge, grandparents herd toddlers in a dialect I cannot understand. It is not a park, but it has become theirs. I imagine they belong to an engineer next door—the kind paid a million yuan to miss dinner.

He works past midnight to give the family a better life. The better life waits downstairs.
B1 / 08:43

First, lipstick.
Then, Crocs.

On day one, I dressed as if Beijing CBD might be watching. Proper trousers. Make-up. Intentions. Within a week, I was wearing Crocs. Shenzhen had defeated me through footwear.

Adaptation can look exactly like surrender. Sometimes it is simply Tuesday.
WHY HERE

I was not sent
to Shenzhen.
I chose it.

I left media for an MBA, then joined a Chinese company going global. I wanted to move closer to money, seniority and the kind of success other people could recognise. It would be dishonest to mock conventional success while quietly checking how much everyone else was earning.

This is not a story about an artist accidentally entering a factory. I opened the door myself. The product simply looked different from the advertisement.
BORDER
THIS SIDETHAT SIDE

Hong Kong is
one checkpoint
away.

One weekend I went to Hong Kong. Across the border, I suddenly wanted to dress properly again. I noticed what people wore, whether they exercised, whether their faces still had some life in them. Comparing a Hong Kong weekend with a Shenzhen workday is obviously cheating. Everyone looks better when they are not at work.

Coming back that evening, the familiar exhaustion returned before the metro doors closed. The cities were one checkpoint apart. Apparently, so were two versions of me.
23:06

Human
match-three.

At 11 p.m., the men on the metro resemble a match-three game: glasses, phones, soft middles, the faint shine of overtime. This is unkind. I probably look no better—Crocs, phone, a face requesting no further human contact. Match any three to clear the level. Unfortunately, none of us clears the level.

Perhaps they, like me, are online complaining about the opposite sex.
SELECT THREE TO CLEAR
INTERNAL COURT

Everyone else
can take it.
Why can't you?

The hardest part was not always the job. It was the trial running inside my head: too picky, too ordinary, too demanding; wants money, freedom and admiration at the same time. Court remained in session around the clock. I played judge, prosecutor and defendant.

I hated writing reports for management, yet spent every evening preparing one for the manager in my head.
MEMORY

Bonsoir.

Coming back from Hong Kong made me think of Paris: saying bonsoir to a neighbour at dusk, still noticing when the sky changed colour. That comparison is even less fair. I was studying then. I did not have to earn my living from Paris. Comparing student life there with office life in Shenzhen is rigged from the start.

Still, I may not miss Paris as much as I miss the version of myself who had enough energy left to notice an evening.
WHAT REMAINS

The media title left.
The reporter
did not.

I have regretted leaving media. Not only because the title sounded respectable. I genuinely liked going out to find a story, talking until a conversation became human, then following one detail far enough to see what the polished statement was hiding.

I may not miss the institution. I miss the person who still believed a difficult question was worth asking.
BURNOUT

“Does it
have to be
tonight?”

At eight one evening, I took the lift down to throw away the rubbish. A woman beside me was negotiating with a voice from work. She was technically outside. Work had declined to notice.

We were all descending in the same metal box. Some of us had simply learned not to feel the brake.

RUB WITH A FINGER OR CURSOR ↗

I want money.
I also want
a life.

I still want money—not in the polite “money isn't everything” sense. I want enough to choose whether I work, which country I live in, and whether marriage or children belong in my life. But if earning it first removes the part of me that writes, talks and remains curious about people, who exactly is the freedom being purchased for?
